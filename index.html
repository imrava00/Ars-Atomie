About the competition

A chemistry olympiad is not a school test with harder numbers. It asks you to combine concepts, read carefully, and reason through problems you have never seen before, usually against a strict clock.

What makes it different

Olympiad questions are layered. A single problem can demand balancing an equation, converting units, and reasoning about a reaction's behavior in sequence. Memorization alone will not carry you; understanding why reactions behave the way they do will.

What the exam looks like

Expect multiple choice and structured questions covering the major fields of chemistry, with formulas, equations, and experimental data presented the way they appear in real scientific work. Time management is a scored skill, which is why every simulation here can run with a live countdown.

The four major fields of chemistry

Olympiad content draws from the four classical branches of the science. A strong competitor is comfortable moving between all of them, because real problems rarely stay inside one field.

Physical chemistry

The why behind reactions: energetics and thermodynamics, reaction kinetics, equilibrium, electrochemistry, and the behavior of atoms and molecules. This field supplies the quantitative backbone of most olympiad calculations.

Analytical chemistry

How we determine what is present and how much of it: titrations, gravimetric and instrumental analysis, spectroscopy, and separation methods. Olympiad problems often hand you experimental data and ask what it proves.

Organic chemistry

The chemistry of carbon: functional groups, reaction mechanisms, isomerism, and synthesis routes. Expect questions that follow a molecule through several transformations and ask you to predict the outcome.

Inorganic chemistry

The rest of the periodic table: periodic trends, main group elements, transition metals, and coordination compounds. This is where structure, bonding, and properties meet the periodic law.

How to prepare with Ars Atomie

Three steps, in order. Repeat the cycle until your score stops improving; that is the signal that you have mastered a topic and are ready for the next one.

1
Study the material

Open the lecture notes and work through the sections your teacher published. Read a topic fully before attempting any simulation on it; the notes are your foundation.

2
Practice with simulations

Open the simulations, pick one, enter your name, and begin. If a time limit is set, the clock behaves exactly like the real contest, so train your pacing as deliberately as your knowledge.

3
Review every miss

After submitting, study the explanation for each wrong answer. Formulas and equations are rendered as properly typeset chemistry, so you can see exactly where your reasoning failed. Then retake the simulation and confirm the lesson stuck.

Frequently asked

Are the simulations graded?
Yes. Each question carries a point value, and your total score appears immediately after submitting, followed by a full review with the answer key.
Do I need an account?
No. Just enter your name before starting a simulation so your teacher can identify your result.
Where are my past results?
Results are saved in the browser you used, on that device. To keep a permanent record, take a screenshot of your result page after submitting.
Can I retake a simulation?
Yes, as many times as you want. Retaking after reading the explanations is the single most effective way to use this site.
Will I need to type chemical formulas?
No. All questions are multiple choice. Formulas and equations are shown as properly typeset chemistry, with correct subscripts and reaction arrows, so you read them exactly as they would appear on a real exam paper.
The formulas look strange on my screen
The rendering library loads from the internet the first time a page opens. Reload the page once while online and everything will display correctly.
